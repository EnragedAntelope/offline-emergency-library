# Map and compass - navigation without a device

**Keep a paper topographic map, a compass, and a printed copy of this guide.**
An offline map on a dead phone is no help. Print maps before an outage, including
the legend, scale, contour interval and north/declination information. Carry a
pencil, straightedge or baseplate compass, and a protractor. Protect paper from rain.
Practice on familiar ground before depending on these skills.

This guide works without electricity, GPS or internet once printed. For the
library's digital maps, see [How to view maps](HOW-TO-VIEW-MAPS.html).

## 1. Read a topographic map

- **Scale:** at 1:25,000, 1 cm on paper represents 250 m on the ground.
  Measure a winding route in short segments with paper or string, then use the
  scale bar. Ground travel over slopes is longer than the horizontal map distance.
  If a printout was resized, use its printed scale bar, not a ruler conversion
  based on the original ratio.
- **Contours:** each line joins points of equal elevation. Read the contour
  interval and elevation units in the margin. Closely spaced lines mean steep
  ground; widely spaced lines mean gentler slopes. Count intervals between labelled
  contours to estimate height change.
- **Terrain:** closed contours with increasing heights indicate a hill; inward
  tick marks indicate a depression. Where contours cross a stream valley, their
  V shapes point uphill/upstream. Compare ridges, valleys, saddles and slopes with
  what you can actually see.
- **Symbols:** read this map's legend for roads, trails, water and buildings.
  A mapped bridge or trail may have disappeared; a blue stream is not proof of
  drinkable water or a safe crossing.

Mark your starting point and destination. Break the route into short legs with
recognizable checkpoints. Prefer clear linear features such as a safe trail or
ridge as **handrails**. Choose a distinct feature beyond the destination as a
**catching feature** so you notice an overshoot. Record distances, turns, hazards
and an escape route. A straight bearing through a cliff or river is not a route.

## 2. Match the map's north to the compass

A bearing (azimuth) is measured clockwise: north 0 or 360 degrees, east 90,
south 180, west 270. Always label its reference:

- **True north:** toward the geographic North Pole.
- **Grid north:** along the map's north-south grid lines.
- **Magnetic north:** the compass needle's north-seeking direction.

**Magnetic declination** is the angle between true and magnetic north. It changes
with place and time. Before leaving, obtain the current local value and date,
write them on the map, and read its north diagram and conversion notes. An old
map's printed magnetic value may be stale. The angle between **grid and magnetic
north** can differ from true-magnetic declination; use the angle for the north
reference you actually measure from. Do not assume the page edge is grid north.

For an **unadjusted compass**, let `D` be magnetic north's angle east of your
chosen map north: east is positive, west is negative.

- Map bearing to magnetic bearing: `magnetic = map - D`.
- Magnetic bearing to map bearing: `map = magnetic + D`.
- Add or subtract 360 until the result is between 0 and 359 degrees.

Worked examples (illustrative values, not your local declination):

| Map-to-magnetic angle | Map bearing | Set on unadjusted compass | Reverse check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 degrees east (`D = +10`) | 90 | 80 | `80 + 10 = 90` |
| 10 degrees west (`D = -10`) | 90 | 100 | `100 - 10 = 90` |
| 10 degrees east (`D = +10`) | 5 | 355 | `355 + 10 = 365`, or 5 |

If your compass has adjustable declination, follow its instructions for the
chosen north reference and **do not apply the correction twice**. The remaining
steps assume an unadjusted baseplate compass and manual conversion.

To orient the map, identify a north-south line for your chosen map reference.
Convert its north bearing (0 degrees) to magnetic using the rule above. Set that
bearing at the compass's direction-of-travel index. Lay the compass edge along
the line with the travel arrow toward map north. Rotate the map and compass
together until the needle's north end lies in the bezel's north orienting arrow.
Check that visible landmarks now lie in the same directions as on the map.

## 3. Take and follow a bearing

**To measure a visible landmark:** stand clear of vehicles, steel fences, magnets
and electrical equipment. Hold the compass level, point the travel arrow at the
landmark, and turn the bezel until its orienting arrow matches the needle's
north end. Read degrees at the travel index. That is a magnetic bearing; convert
it before plotting it on the map. Move away and recheck if readings seem unstable.

**To plan a leg on paper:**

1. Put the compass's straight edge from your known position **toward** the next
   checkpoint, travel arrow pointing toward the checkpoint.
2. Without rotating the baseplate, turn the bezel until its north-south lines
   parallel the chosen map north lines, with bezel north toward map north.
   Read the map bearing at the index. A protractor aligned to the same map north
   is another way to measure it.
3. Convert to magnetic and set that number at the compass index.
4. Lift the compass, keep it level, and turn your body until the north needle
   sits inside the orienting arrow. The travel arrow now points along the leg.
   Check the **north** end, not the south end, to avoid a 180-degree reversal.
5. Pick a recognizable object on that line, lower the compass and walk safely
   to it. Stop and repeat. Do not walk while staring at the compass.

Track elapsed time and distance, for example by a pace count calibrated over a
known distance. Slopes, vegetation and fatigue change your pace. Confirm each
checkpoint against terrain before starting the next leg. If an obstacle forces
a detour, use a safe mapped route and re-establish your position and bearing;
holding the original compass angle after a sideways detour does not put you back
on the original line.

A **back bearing** is the opposite direction: add 180 degrees if below 180,
otherwise subtract 180. For example, 80 becomes 260; 260 becomes 80.
This reverses a direction, not every turn or obstacle in your route.

## 4. Find your position: triangulation / resection

Finding yourself from bearings to known landmarks is commonly called
triangulation; the Army calls this **resection**. You need at least two correctly
identified, well-separated landmarks visible both on the ground and on the map.
A third gives a useful error check. Avoid landmarks nearly in the same or
opposite directions, where the plotted lines meet at a shallow angle.

1. Remain at one observation point. Identify and mark the landmarks on the map.
2. Measure the magnetic bearing **from you to** the first landmark.
3. Convert it to the map's north reference, then calculate its back bearing.
4. From that landmark's mapped position, use the protractor to draw the back
   bearing line toward your possible position.
5. Repeat for the other landmarks. Where the lines cross is your estimated
   position. Three imperfect readings usually form a small triangle.

Example with `D = +10`: a landmark seen at 80 magnetic is at 90 map; draw
its 270-degree back bearing from the landmark toward you.

The error triangle is an uncertainty area, not proof that its center is safe.
Compare it with ridges, streams and your last known position. If it is large,
recheck identification, north reference, conversions and compass interference.
If you are definitely on a mapped trail, one landmark's back-bearing line can
help locate you where it crosses that trail; multiple crossings remain ambiguous.

## 5. Natural navigation when the compass is unavailable

These methods estimate **true** directions, not magnetic or grid bearings.
Use several clues together and compare them with terrain. They cannot locate
you on their own and are not precise enough for a hazardous crossing.

### Sun

The sun generally rises in the eastern part of the sky and sets in the western
part; due east/west applies near the equinoxes, not year-round. At local solar
noon it is generally south in northern temperate latitudes and north in southern
temperate latitudes. Clock noon may differ from solar noon; within the tropics
the noon sun can be on either side or overhead. Do not assume "sun = south"
everywhere, or use this shortcut in polar conditions.

### Shadow-stick

1. On level ground in sunlight, plant a straight stick upright. Mark its shadow
   tip with a stone.
2. Wait about 10-15 minutes without moving the stick; mark the new shadow tip.
3. Draw a line from the **first** mark toward the **second**. Treat this as a
   rough west-to-east line.
4. With the first mark on your left and second on your right, face roughly
   north; south is behind you.

This short-interval method is approximate: season, latitude and time of day
affect it. It requires a distinct shadow and level ground; it is not suitable
in polar regions (above about 60 degrees north or south in the Army guidance).
Do not turn a rough shadow line into a precise travel bearing.

### Stars

- **Northern Hemisphere:** locate the Big Dipper's two bowl stars opposite the
  handle. Follow the line from the outer bowl's bottom star through its rim star
  about five times their separation to Polaris, at the end of the Little
  Dipper's handle. A line down from Polaris to the horizon gives approximate
  true north. Polaris is not the brightest star; confirm the constellation.
- **Southern Hemisphere:** identify the Southern Cross, not a look-alike.
  Extend its long axis from the top star through the foot about 4.5 times
  the cross's length beyond the foot. Drop a line from that estimated sky
  point to the horizon for approximate true south.

Clouds, terrain, latitude and season can hide these patterns. Learn them before
travel; do not guess from a single unfamiliar star. Moss or the shape of one
tree is not a dependable compass.

## 6. When the observations disagree

Stop moving and keep the group together. Check the last confirmed location,
map scale, landmark identity, compass interference and north conversion.
Do not walk farther on a guess. If you cannot confidently recover your position,
seek a safe nearby place, shelter and signal for help rather than wandering.
Keep your paper map and route notes accessible even when electronics are working.
